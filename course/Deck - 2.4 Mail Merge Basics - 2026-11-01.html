Warm-up · type in the chat

C · "Dear Colleague"

One letter for all, no names.

D · Something faster

One letter plus one list.

Ask them to type A, B, C or D in the chat. Many will honestly say B; that's what most offices do.

Ask: "With B, what usually goes wrong?" Expect answers like a wrong name left over from the previous copy, or "Dear Maria" on a letter for Pedro. That's embarrassing on an official letter.

D is today's answer: mail merge. Same quality as typing each one, in a fraction of the time, and no leftover names.

The big idea

Mail merge works like party loot bags.

  • One design for every bag: that's your letter.
  • One list of the kids who are coming: that's your data source.
  • A blank name tag on each bag: that's the merge field. Each bag gets a different name.
Result: one letter + one list = many personalised letters, in one file.

Use the birthday party analogy because everyone has prepared loot bags for a kid's party. You don't design a new bag for each child; you make one design and stick a name tag on each.

Mail merge is exactly that: the letter is designed once, the list holds the names, and the merge field is the empty name tag that Word fills in, one recipient at a time.

Ask: "If a child's name is misspelled on the list, what happens to the tag?" Right, it's wrong on the bag too. Clean list, clean letters.

Before you print or send

Preview, then check like a proofreader

  1. Look at the first, last and one middle letter, not just the first.
  2. Check spaces and punctuation around every field: "Dear Ana Reyes:" not "DearAna Reyes :".
  3. Check the record count: 3 people in the list should mean 3 letters.
  4. Use Edit Recipient List to untick anyone who should not get a letter.
  5. Print one test page before printing the whole batch.

Preview is your last chance to catch mistakes before 40 letters go out with the same error.

Why first, last and middle? Errors in the list often sit at the end: a blank row, a half-typed name. Checking only the first letter misses them.

Show Edit Recipient List in Word: you can untick a person without deleting them from the list. Useful when someone has already confirmed by phone. And always print one test page; paper and toner are budget items.

Troubleshooting

Four common mail merge errors

What you seeLikely causeFix
Wrong names, or no names at allYou picked the wrong sheet or table in Select Table.Select Recipients again and choose the sheet that holds the list.
Blank letters at the endEmpty rows, or rows with only a space, in the list.Delete empty rows in the sheet, or untick them in Edit Recipient List.
Numbers lose their formatWord takes the raw value: 1500 instead of ₱1,500.00.Type the amount as text in the sheet, or fix the format in Word before merging.
Dates look differentA date like 19 November 2026 may show as 11/19/2026.Type the date as text in the sheet, or keep the date in the main letter instead.

These four cause most of the "it doesn't work" messages.

Wrong sheet: easy fix, reconnect. Blank records: a stray space in row 20 is enough to make an extra empty letter.

Numbers and dates: Word reads the underlying value from the spreadsheet, not the formatting you see. For beginners the simplest fix is to type amounts and dates as text in the sheet, or keep fixed details like the workshop date in the letter itself. Advanced users can use field formatting codes; we won't need them for the lab.

Quick check · click an answer

Your list has 3 names, but the merged file has 5 letters and 2 are blank. Most likely cause?

A
The font in the letter is too small
No. Font size changes how the letter looks, not how many letters are made.
B
The spreadsheet has 2 empty rows inside the list
Yes. Each row, even an empty one, becomes a letter. Delete it or untick it.
C
The margins are not 1 inch
No. Margins affect the page layout, not the number of records.
D
You forgot to click Preview Results
No. Preview only shows the letters; it doesn't add or remove any.

Let them answer in the chat first, then click to reveal. B: each row in the data source becomes one letter, even an empty-looking one with a stray space.
