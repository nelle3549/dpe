Quick check
You made the logo smaller and now it looks squashed. What happened?
AThe file is corrupted. Insert it again from the internet.
BThe wrapping must be changed to Behind Text.
CA side handle was dragged. Undo, then resize from a corner.
DThe zoom is wrong. Zoom out until it looks normal.
Side handles stretch one way; corners keep the proportions. Wrapping and zoom don't change the picture's shape.

Our letter says the workshop runs from 8:30 to 4:30.
But readers will ask:
what happens when, and where?
A table answers that at a glance.
Today we add a schedule table to our invitation,
then a logo letterhead, with pictures that look professional,
not stretched.
Think of the fare matrix in a jeepney. You find your distance one way,
the passenger type the other way, and read where they meet.
That's a table.
Rows run across, like rows of seats in a church.
Columns stand up, like the pillars.
A cell is where a row and a column meet.
And the header row labels each column.
Count before you click.
Our schedule needs three columns, Time,
Activity and Venue, and six rows:
a header and five time slots.
After the line Here is the schedule,
click Insert, Table, and drag across three by six.
Type Time, then press Tab to jump to the next cell.
Tab in the last cell adds a new row.
But Enter makes the row taller instead of moving on.
Oops, we forgot lunch.
Click the Word processing row, right-click, and choose Insert, Rows Below.
Type the lunch break. To remove a row,
right-click and choose Delete.
The Delete key only empties the cells.
For a title across the whole table, select the top row's cells,
right-click, and choose Merge Cells.
Use merges sparingly.
Now make it readable.
Double-click the border between two columns, and the column fits its longest entry.
Select the header row and make it bold and centered.
Click inside the table, open Table Design,
and pick a style, or just shade the header.
Keep it light: dark shading wastes ink and blurs on a photocopy.
For amounts, right-align so the digits line up.
Next, pictures.
Click Insert, Pictures, This Device.
Drag a side handle and faces stretch, and a squashed logo looks careless.
So drag a corner handle: same shape, just smaller.
Then click Layout Options.
In Line with Text sits in the line like a
big letter, the safest choice for letters.
Square wraps text around the sides,
nice for invitations.
Now a simple letterhead.
Open the header: double-click the top margin.
Insert a two-column table and set Borders to No Border.
Put the logo in the left cell,
resized from a corner, and the office name in the right.
Add a bottom border line and close the header.
It repeats on every page by itself.
Use your office's logo only as your office allows.
A few finishing touches.
Right-click a table or picture and choose Insert Caption.
The numbers update by themselves.
Table captions go above, figure captions below.
Add alt text so colleagues who use screen readers know
what the picture shows. And for a diagram, like the steps of a transaction,
use Shapes or SmartArt:
three to five boxes, no 3D.
At the office, you'll use tables for schedules,
duty rosters and transmittal lists, where every document received gets
a row with the date and who received it.
At home, try a weekly meal plan with Day,
Ulam and Who cooks, a chore chart for the ref,
or a birthday invitation with one photo, wrapped Square and resized from a corner.
Quick check.
You made the logo smaller, and now it looks squashed.
Is the file corrupted?
Is it the wrapping? Was a side handle dragged?
Or is it the zoom? Think about it for a moment. The answer:
a side handle was dragged.
Undo, then resize from a corner.
Wrapping changes how text flows, and zoom only changes your
view.
So, plan the columns and Tab between cells.
Keep them readable with a bold header and a light style.
Resize pictures from a corner, and put the logo in the header.
