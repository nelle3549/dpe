Why charts

A good chart answers one question at a glance.

  • Compare: which item, section or month is biggest?
  • See a trend: are supply costs going up or down?
  • Show a share: how much of the budget goes to food?
  • Spot the odd one: a month that suddenly jumps stands out.
Rule of thumb: before you make a chart, write the one question it should answer. That question becomes the title.

Charts are not decoration. Each one should answer one question: compare, trend, share, or outlier.

Share a real moment: a section chief flipping through a report has maybe five seconds per page. A chart with a clear title gets read; a dense table gets skipped. Ask the chat: "What's one report in your section that would be easier as a chart?"

Pick the right chart

Three charts cover most office reports

Column / Bar · compare

Items, sections or people side by side. Use a bar (sideways) when the labels are long.

Line · trend over time

Months, weeks or days in order. Shows rises, dips and seasons.

Pie · parts of one whole

Shares that add up to 100%. Keep it to about 6 slices or fewer.

Three questions, three charts. Compare: column or bar. Trend over time: line. Share of one whole: pie.

Two tips: use a horizontal bar when labels are long like "Envelope, brown long", because they fit on the left. And a pie only works when the slices add up to one meaningful total, like one month's budget, and when there are few slices. More than about six and the small ones become unreadable.

Step 1 · select the data

Labels + values only. Leave out the TOTAL row.

  1. Drag over A1:A9 (the item names, with the header).
  2. Hold Ctrl and drag over E1:E9 (the totals). Mac: Cmd.
  3. Check: both cover the same rows, 1 to 9. Row 10 stays out: the TOTAL is not an item.

Most chart problems start here. We want two things: the labels in column A and the values in column E. Columns B to D are not needed for this chart, so we skip them with Ctrl.

Include the headers in row 1; the spreadsheet uses "Total Cost" as the series name. Leave row 10 out. Demo the Ctrl-drag slowly; people often let go of Ctrl too early and lose the first selection.

Step 2 · insert

Insert the chart in your app

  1. Data still selected: Insert › Recommended Charts, or pick Column / Bar / Line / Pie.
  2. The chart appears on the sheet.
  3. Click it: the Chart Design tab opens.

Google Sheets: Insert › Chart opens the Chart editor (Setup, Customize). LibreOffice Calc: Insert › Chart… starts the Chart Wizard.

Windows shortcut: Alt + F1 drops a default chart; F11 puts it on its own sheet. Wrong type? Change it later.

With the range still selected, insert the chart. Show it in Excel first. Recommended Charts is helpful for beginners: Excel previews a few that fit the data.

Step 3 · label it

The parts of a chart

  1. Chart title · required in Lab 3 (not the default text)
  2. Axis titles · what and which unit (₱)
  3. Data labels · the numbers on the columns
  4. Legend · only when there are 2+ series. Add each part: Chart Design › Add Chart Element (Windows also: the + button).

Sheets: Chart editor › Customize. Calc: double-click the chart, then Insert.

Tidy up

Move it, resize it, change its type

  1. Move: drag the chart by its border, not the bars. Place it beside the table.
  2. Resize: drag a corner handle until every label reads.
  3. Change type: Chart Design › Change Chart Type. Sheets: Chart editor › Setup › Chart type. Calc: right-click the chart in edit mode › Chart Type.
  4. Switch Row/Column swaps items and series; Move Chart puts it on its own sheet.
Delete the legend when there is only one series: click it, press Delete.

Three quick moves. Demo moving by grabbing the outer border: if you grab a bar, you select the series instead.

Then change our column chart into a bar chart, and back, to show nothing is lost. Finally, delete the legend that says "Total Cost": with one series it repeats the title. In Google Sheets you set Legend to "None" under Customize, Legend.

Pie chart · at home

Share of one monthly budget

  • The 8 budget lines were grouped into 6; select A1:B7, no total.
  • The slices add up to 100% of one ₱20,250 budget.
  • Show percentages as data labels: Food is 37%.
  • Keep the legend: it names the slices.
About 6 slices max. More? Group the small ones, or use a bar chart.

Our home example: the same ₱20,250 monthly household budget from 3.3, with eight lines. Eight slices is too many, so we grouped rice with groceries as Food and the CEPALCO bill with water as Utilities. Now six slices.

Food is the biggest at 37%, then utilities at 16%. Ask: "What would your family's biggest slice be?" A pie works here because every slice is part of the same whole. You'd never make a pie of eight unrelated monthly totals.

Budget vs Actual

Two series? Now you need the legend.

  • Select three columns: Item, Budget, Actual.
  • A clustered column puts each pair side by side.
  • The legend tells Budget (blue) from Actual (red), Excel's default colours.

Groceries went ₱480 over, CEPALCO ₱320, fare ₱240, rice ₱150. Total: ₱21,210 against a ₱20,250 budget, ₱960 over.

When you compare two sets of numbers, budget and actual, the legend becomes essential. Without it, nobody knows which bar is which.

Read the story in the chart: groceries, CEPALCO, fare and rice went over budget; school allowance and water came in a little under. Altogether the family spent ₱21,210 against a ₱20,250 budget: ₱960 over. The tallest gold bars point straight at where to cut. Ask if anyone tracks budget vs actual at home.

Line chart · trend

Months in order, one line

  • Months in order in the table: a line follows the row order.
  • Time goes on the horizontal axis, left to right.
  • The line shows the summer peak in April and May, then a drop in June. Axis titles say what and which unit.

A line is for change over time. These are sample monthly bills for a household: ₱2,650 in January rising to ₱3,600 in May, when every electric fan and aircon is running, then ₱3,150 in June.

Office version: monthly supplies spending, or documents received per week. Make sure the months are in order in your table; a line chart follows the order of the rows.

Common mistakes

Three charts that tell the wrong story

TOTAL row in the pie

The TOTAL slice is always exactly half the pie. Select rows 2 to 9 only.

Axis starts at 6,000

Starts at 6,000: looks 15× bigger

Starts at 0: about 1.3× bigger

Bar and column axes should start at zero.

3D effects

Tilting makes front slices look bigger than they are. Use flat 2D charts.

Also avoid: more than ~6 pie slices · "Chart Title" left as is · no axis titles · a pie of unrelated numbers.

Go card by card. One: if you select row 10 with the items, the TOTAL equals the sum of all other slices, so it always takes exactly half the pie. Two: long paper costs 7,950 and A4 costs 6,125, about 1.3 times more. Start the axis at 6,000 and the bar looks 15 times bigger. Spreadsheets sometimes do this automatically; check the axis.

Three: 3D charts look fancy but distort sizes. Keep it flat and simple.

Quick check · click an answer

You select A1:A10 and E1:E10 and insert a pie. One slice is exactly half the pie. Why?

A
Long paper is half of all spending
No. Long paper is about 36% of ₱22,039.00.
B
Row 10, the TOTAL, became a slice
Yes. The total equals all other slices combined, so it takes 50%. Select rows 1 to 9.
C
Pie charts always enlarge the first slice
No. A flat pie draws each slice in proportion to its value.
D
The chart needs a legend to fix it
No. A legend labels the slices; it doesn't change their size.

Chat first, then click. Answer: B.

This is the most common Lab 3 chart error. The fix is simply selecting rows 1 to 9. In Excel you can also fix it after the fact: Chart Design, Select Data, and edit the range. Long paper's real share is 7,950 ÷ 22,039, about 36%.
