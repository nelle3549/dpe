Next · 1.2 Desktop and File Management
Every computer you use, from the desktop at your BIR
office to the phone in your pocket, is a computer system.
And every computer system, big or small,
has the same three parts:
hardware, software and
peopleware. Let's look at each one, and see why the third
part matters most.
Think of a jeepney. The hardware is the jeep itself:
the engine, the wheels, the seats.
You can touch it.
The software is the route and the rules:
where to go, and when to stop.
You can't touch it, but nothing moves without it.
And the peopleware is the driver,
the barker and the passengers.
Without them, the jeep just sits in the terminal.
Let's start with hardware.
Every piece of hardware does one of four jobs.
Input devices put data in: the keyboard where you type a TIN,
or the scanner for a signed form. The system unit processes it.
Inside, the CPU is the brain,
and the RAM is its working desk.
Output devices show the result:
the monitor, the printer, the speakers.
And storage keeps your files after you switch off:
the hard drive, a flash drive,
or the cloud.
Here's how they work together on a real task: the daily summary report.
You type the day's figures and scan the request letter. That's input.
The CPU adds the totals in a split second.
That's processing.
The report appears on your monitor,
then prints for signature.
That's output.
Then you save it to the shared drive,
so the next shift can find it. That's storage.
So when something fails, ask:
which step broke?
A blank printout is an output problem.
A file you can't find is a storage problem.
Part two is software:
the instructions that tell the hardware what to do.
There are two kinds.
System software runs the machine itself:
Windows, macOS, Android, even the printer driver.
Application software does a specific job for you:
the word processor for a letter,
the spreadsheet for your monthly report, the web browser, and
eBIRForms. One rule here:
install only licensed,
office-approved software.
Pirated and unknown apps are a common way viruses get
in.
Part three is peopleware, and it's the most important part.
End users, like encoders, examiners, admin staff,
and you, use the system to do the work.
The ICT focal person keeps the hardware and software running.
Developers and system administrators build and manage the systems.
And data owners decide who may see or change information.
The best computer in the region is useless if nobody
uses it well.
Some books add a fourth element:
data. The figures you type, the forms you scan,
the taxpayer records you look up.
Hardware, software and people all exist to handle data.
That's why protecting it is part of everyone's job.
These same three parts are everywhere.
At the office, the desktop PC, the scanner and the network printer are hardware.
Windows, email and eBIRForms are software.
And you, your section chief and the ICT focal person
are the peopleware. At home, it's your smartphone, the Wi-Fi router and the
smart TV. Android, GCash and Messenger.
And you, your kids doing online classes,
and the internet provider's technician.
Quick check.
During a brownout, the UPS beeps and keeps your PC
on. Is the UPS system software, hardware,
application software, or
peopleware? Think about it for a moment.
The answer:
it's hardware.
A UPS is a physical box that supplies backup power.
If you can touch it, it's hardware.
Before we wrap up, five habits that protect your hardware
and your work. One: save often with Control S.
A brownout erases unsaved work.
Two: plug the PC into a UPS, not straight into the wall.
Three: shut down properly from the Start menu,
never by pulling the plug. Four:
keep it cool and clean, with no food or drinks near the keyboard.
And five: report problems early to your ICT focal person,
and say which step failed.
So, every computer system needs all three parts.
Hardware you can touch.
Software that gives the instructions.
And peopleware:
you, the part that decides whether the system is used
well. Next, in topic 1.2, we'll put every file where you
can find it again.
