Welcome to Module 4. We've written letters and built spreadsheets.
Now we present what we know.
This video covers the part most people skip: planning and design.
No app yet, just a pen and a sheet of paper.
Plan a deck like a fiesta handaan. Who is coming?
Lolas, kids, the barangay captain:
that's your audience.
Why the party? That's your purpose.
What's the star dish, the lechon everyone remembers?
That's your one key message.
Only then do you choose the decorations:
the design.
So answer three questions first.
Audience, who are they, and what do they need from
you? Purpose, to inform, persuade, train, or ask for a decision?
And key message:
the one sentence they should remember tomorrow.
For Lab 4, that could be:
In 16 hours I went from typing letters to building
charts.
Step two: sketch on paper.
Brain-dump every idea, in any order.
Group similar ideas, and cross out what doesn't serve your
key message.
Put them in order: a hook, the proof, then your message.
Then draw one box per slide, with a title and a rough picture.
On paper, you can't waste time picking fonts.
Here's Lab 4 in five boxes.
A title slide with your name and one photo. A hook: where you started.
Proof one:
your Lab 2 letters, three in one click.
Proof two: the Lab 3 chart.
And a close: your key message and next step. Five boxes, five slides:
that already meets the Lab 4 minimum.
The rule of thumb:
one idea per slide.
This crowded slide crams three modules together,
and its title is only a label. Split it into three focused slides,
each with a title that states the point, like: Formulas do the math.
If you need a second title, you need a second slide.
Step three:
design.
Four principles do most of the work. Contrast, make different things very different,
like dark text on white. Alignment, every item lines up with something.
Repetition, same colours, fonts and title position on every slide.
Proximity, keep related items close, like a caption right below
its photo. Then squint at your slide:
can you still tell what matters?
Keep it readable.
Use two or three colours, plus one accent for what matters.
Use plain fonts like Arial, Calibri,
Verdana or Aptos.
Keep body text at least 24 points.
Many people join online on a phone, so test your slide small.
And for accessibility, never use colour alone to show meaning.
In PowerPoint, Review, Check Accessibility finds missing alt text and
low contrast.
Here's a makeover of a slide that was a wall
of small text, yellow on purple, in three fonts.
The title now states the message: four modules, four new skills.
Four short lines, one font, dark on white.
And the long paragraph moves into the speaker notes,
and into what you say out loud.
Audience and purpose shape the design.
For a section chief, the purpose is a decision,
so the key number and your request go on slide 2:
bond paper is our biggest supply cost,
so we need to reorder this week. For new staff, you train:
steps, photos and who to ask.
And a family reunion slideshow celebrates:
one big photo per slide, and large fonts for the
lolas.
Quick check.
You must brief your section chief in five minutes. What do you decide first:
the theme, the key message and what you need,
the transitions, or the number of slides?
Think about it for a moment. The answer:
the key message and what you need from the chief. Everything else serves it.
So, plan first:
audience, purpose and one key message.
Sketch your storyboard on paper, one idea per slide.
Then design with contrast, alignment, repetition and proximity,
in fonts everyone can read. Keep your paper by the keyboard:
next, in topic 4.2, we turn those boxes into real
slides.
