Where AI goes wrong

Two weak spots to watch for

Hallucination

Confident but wrong
  • A law number that doesn't exist
  • A wrong deadline, a fake quote
  • Rule: check every name, number, date and law

Bias

Unfair patterns copied from data
  • "Siya" becomes "he" for a doctor, "she" for a nurse
  • May know less about local places and Bisaya terms
  • Rule: ask, is this fair to everyone?
AI only knows the examples it was shown. Limited data, limited answers.
Digital Productivity Essentials · 1.5 Everyday AI
The one rule at work

Never paste confidential data into public AI

Never paste

  • Taxpayer names, TINs, addresses, returns
  • Internal memos, case details, office passwords
  • Your family's IDs, bank and e-wallet details

Instead

  • Ask for a template with blanks like [NAME]
  • Fill in the details yourself, offline
  • Follow your office's policy on approved tools
If you wouldn't post it on Facebook, don't paste it into a public AI.

Some people are excited about artificial intelligence.
Others are worried. Both are fair.
In this video, we'll explain AI in plain words,
notice where you already use it,
learn its weak spots, and follow one firm rule about confidential data.
No math or programming needed.
How does a child learn to tell a ripe mango? Not from a rulebook.
She sees many mangoes.
AI learns the same way.
Show it thousands of mango photos labelled ripe or not ripe.
It finds the pattern: color, spots, shape.
Then it makes a guess on a new mango: ripe, ninety-two percent sure.
A calculator always gives the same answer.
AI learned its own rules from examples, so it can be wrong.
Surprise:
you already use AI daily.
Your spam filter learned what junk and scam emails look like.
Your map app predicts traffic from phones on the road.
Face unlock recognizes you, even with new glasses.
Video suggestions and translation apps learn too.
And chatbots like ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini and Meta AI are
the newest kind, called generative AI:
they write new text.
Now, what AI is not. It's not a thinking person:
it sounds friendly because it learned how people write.
It's not always right: it predicts likely answers, and likely is not the same
as true. It's not an official source:
it doesn't replace bir dot gov dot ph or your
chief. And it's not your replacement.
People who use AI well, and check it, become more valuable.
AI has two weak spots.
The first is hallucination:
it invents facts that sound real, like a law number that doesn't exist,
or a wrong deadline. So check every name, number, date and law against an
official source. The second is bias.
AI copies unfair patterns from its examples.
A translator may turn siya into he for a doctor
and she for a nurse. Remember: AI only knows the examples it was shown.
Now the one rule at work.
What you type into a free AI tool may be
stored and reviewed, and once pasted,
you can't take it back. So never paste taxpayer names, TINs or returns,
internal memos, passwords, or your family's bank details.
Use blanks instead, and fill in the details yourself,
offline. If you wouldn't post it on Facebook,
don't paste it into a public AI.
Here's the safe way.
You type a request for a polite template letter about
a missing document, using name and document as blanks.
A draft appears in seconds. Then check it:
is every fact correct?
Edit it into your own words, and own it.
Your name goes on it, so it's your responsibility.
So where does AI help?
At the office:
drafting a generic memo, making an email clearer,
or explaining a spreadsheet formula in simple words. At home:
a week's meal plan within 2,000 pesos,
your child's fractions lesson step by step,
or promo text for the sari-sari store. AI is the
copilot. You're the pilot who lands the plane.
